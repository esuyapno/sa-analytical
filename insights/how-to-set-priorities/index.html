How to Set Priorities:A Practical Guide to an Organisational Strategy Review

Ekci Suyapno25 November 20266 min read

Too many priorities, too little focus

Ask the leadership team of a busy foundation or NGO to name its three main priorities. You will often hear five different answers.

New programmes get added every year, almost none are closed, and the strategic plan turns into an inventory of everything the organisation already does.

Stretched teams follow. Funding gets spread across too many areas, and the board struggles to explain what the organisation actually exists to do. The Bridgespan Group warns against exactly this: plans that try to cover every issue and every stakeholder end up clarifying nothing (Bridgespan, 2025).

An organisational strategy review breaks that pattern. It is not an excuse to rewrite your mission statement. It is a hard look at the evidence, so you can agree on what matters, decide what to grow and choose what to cut.

Signs you need a strategy review

Do not wait for the end of a five-year planning cycle. Run a review as soon as you spot two or more of these warning signs:

  • Funding is spread thin. Too many small programmes, none with enough money to make a real dent.
  • The context has shifted. A funding cut, a sudden crisis, new leadership, or fresh evidence about what communities actually need.
  • Staff cannot name the top three priorities. Worse, they each name different ones.
  • Decisions keep bouncing back to the board. Without shared priorities, every routine choice turns into a debate.
  • Nothing ever stops. New initiatives arrive every year, yet no programme has been closed in living memory.
  • The people you serve are absent from the room. Head office or funders set the agenda, with barely any input from communities and local partners.

A five-step organisational strategy review

A review should not drag on for a year. For most organisations, these five steps fit comfortably into six to ten weeks, anchored by one or two facilitated workshops.

  1. Gather the evidence. Pull together what you already know: programme results, financial records, feedback from the people you serve, and honest views from staff, local partners and the board. A short self-assessment stops people arguing over basic facts.
  2. Revisit your intended impact. Write down, in one or two plain sentences, the exact change you will hold yourselves accountable for, and for whom. Bridgespan draws a sharp line between this intended impact (what you want to achieve) and your theory of change (how you will get there) (Bridgespan, 2020).
  3. List everything you do. Build a single inventory of every programme, project and core function, alongside the money and staff time each one consumes. Most leadership teams have never seen their entire workload on one page. The sheer length of that list usually sparks the real conversation.
  4. Rate every item on the same yardstick. Apply exactly the same questions to every item on the page (see below). This shifts the room away from defending pet projects and towards a fair comparison.
  5. Decide, and decide what to stop. Choose your priorities. Spell out what will grow, what will change and what will end, then put a date in the diary to review progress. Building the “stop” list is easily the hardest part, and the only way any of this works.

How to set priorities: a simple scoring grid

Use a short scoring grid. Pick four or five criteria, score each programme from 1 (low) to 5 (high), and use the numbers to provoke debate rather than treating the final tally as a binding verdict.

CriterionQuestion to ask
Fit with intended impactHow directly does this contribute to the change we exist to make?
Evidence of resultsWhat do we actually know about whether it works, and for whom?
Relevance to communitiesDo the people it serves see it as a real priority?
Unique contributionWould this happen without us, and are we best placed to run it?
SustainabilityCan the results last through local ownership and resources?

The grid will not make the decision for you. Its real value lies in the arguments it surfaces: why did one director score a programme 2 while another gave it 5? Those clashes usually expose the true strategic fault lines.

If one criterion matters more than the rest, agree on its weight before scoring begins, so nobody tweaks the maths later to rescue a favourite project.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Reviewing strategy without touching resources. Priorities that fail to shift money and staff time are just wishful thinking.
  • Letting the loudest voice win. Lock in your criteria first, so the room debates evidence rather than deferring to seniority.
  • Leaving out the people the work is for. Communities and local partners spot blind spots that head office misses. Bring them in at the start, not as a rubber-stamp consultation at the end.
  • Skipping the “stop” list. If nothing closes, your new priorities simply pile on top of an already unmanageable workload.
  • Treating the review as a one-off event. Book a check-in six or twelve months ahead to test whether your choices are holding up.

Where to start

Start small. Put together a one-page inventory of everything you do, then ask your leadership team to write down the three things the organisation must get right over the next two years.

Lay those lists side by side. Where they clash, your review begins.
