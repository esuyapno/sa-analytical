Theory of Change for Funders:A Practical Template

Ekci Suyapno27 November 20267 min read

Funders ask for theories of change. Few write their own.

Most foundations ask grantees for a theory of change. Far fewer have one for themselves.

That gap is not new. The UK think tank NPC made the case more than a decade ago that funders can use the same tool to plan, review and evaluate their own work (NPC, 2014). The result is a portfolio of well-reasoned projects that do not add up to a clear strategy: each grant makes sense on its own, but nobody can explain how they combine to change anything.

A funder’s theory of change fixes this. It sets out what change the foundation wants to see, how its grants and other resources are meant to contribute, and what has to be true for that to happen. It also gives the board a clear basis for saying yes, and for saying no.

This article explains what a theory of change is, how a funder’s version differs from a grantee’s, and offers a simple template you can use with your team.

What a theory of change is (and is not)

Put simply, a theory of change is your organisation’s reasoning, written down. It starts from the change you want to see, traces back through everything that must shift first, and names the beliefs that hold each link in that chain together.

The approach took shape among evaluators in the mid-1990s. Carol Weiss, then working with the Aspen Institute’s Roundtable on Community Change, saw that ambitious community programmes were so hard to assess because nobody had stated clearly what they expected to happen, or why (Center for Theory of Change). Writing those expectations down turns them into something you can check.

It is worth being clear about what it is not:

  • Not a logframe. A logframe lists outputs, indicators and targets for one project. A theory of change explains the reasoning behind them, and works at any level.
  • Not a diagram for a proposal. If it is drawn once and never revisited, it is decoration.
  • Not a promise of attribution. Most change has many causes. A good theory of change describes your contribution, not sole credit.

How a funder’s theory of change differs

A grantee’s theory of change explains how its programme leads to change for the people it serves. A funder’s theory of change works one level up: it explains how the foundation’s choices (what to fund, whom to fund, how to fund) contribute to a broader change.

Grantee theory of changeFunder theory of change
LevelOne programme or organisationA portfolio, a strategy area or the whole foundation
Main leversActivities and servicesGrants, partnerships, convening, advocacy, investment
Key questionDoes our programme work?Are we funding the right things, in the right way?
Typical assumptionsPeople will use the service; behaviour will changeGrantees have the capacity to deliver; our funding model helps rather than hinders them

That last row matters. A funder’s own practices, such as short grant terms, restricted budgets or heavy reporting, are part of its theory of change, whether written down or not.

A theory of change template for funders

Work through the template from top to bottom. Start with the long-term change and work backwards, rather than starting from the grants you already make. One page per strategy area is usually enough.

ComponentGuiding questions
1. The problemWhat problem are we trying to address? Who is most affected, and how do they describe it?
2. Long-term changeWhat will be different in 10 years if we succeed? For whom?
3. Intermediate outcomesWhat needs to change first, in people’s lives, in organisations or in systems, for the long-term change to happen?
4. Our roleWhich of these outcomes can we realistically influence? What do others already do?
5. Our leversWhich grants, partnerships, convening or advocacy will we use? How will we fund (multi-year, flexible, restricted)?
6. AssumptionsWhat must be true for each step to lead to the next? Which assumptions are we least sure of?
7. EvidenceWhat evidence supports our logic? Where are the gaps?
8. Signals of progressWhich few indicators will tell us whether we are on track, and which decisions will they inform?
9. VoicesHow have communities, grantees and local partners shaped this theory of change?

Write each component in plain language. If a board member or a community partner cannot follow it, simplify it.

Theory of change example: a filled-in template

Below is a short, illustrative example for a fictional foundation funding youth employment in two secondary cities. Yours will be specific to your context, but it should be about this length.

ComponentExample
1. The problemYoung people aged 18–24 in two secondary cities face high unemployment. They tell us training rarely leads to a job, and that employers do not trust informal skills.
2. Long-term changeIn 10 years, young people in both cities, including young women and those without secondary school certificates, find decent, stable work.
3. Intermediate outcomesLocal employers recruit through youth-led and community organisations; training is shaped by employer demand; local authorities fund what works.
4. Our roleWe cannot create jobs. We can strengthen local organisations, connect them with employers, and help build the evidence local authorities need.
5. Our leversFive-year, flexible core grants to 6–8 local organisations; a yearly employer convening; co-funding a shared tracking study with the city authorities.
6. AssumptionsEmployers will hire through local partners if quality is consistent (least certain); flexible funding lets partners adapt; authorities will act on local evidence.
7. EvidenceStrong evidence that demand-led training improves job outcomes; weak evidence on employer behaviour in these two cities.
8. Signals of progressShare of participants in work after 12 months; number of employers recruiting through partners. Reviewed yearly to decide whether to scale or adjust.
9. VoicesDrafted with partner organisations and a youth advisory group; tested with employers before board approval.

Illustrative example, not a real foundation.

Note how the riskiest assumption is flagged, and how each signal of progress is tied to a decision.

Common mistakes, and how to avoid them

  • Working forwards from existing grants. This produces a justification of the current portfolio, not a strategy. Start from the change you want.
  • Hiding the assumptions. The arrows between boxes are where strategies fail. Write each assumption down and mark the riskiest ones.
  • Imposing your theory of change on grantees. Your theory of change should leave room for grantees’ own logic. Ask how their work connects to your outcomes, rather than requiring them to adopt your framework.
  • Writing it without the people it concerns. Communities and local organisations understand the problem and its causes in ways a foundation office cannot. Involve them from the first draft, not only at validation.
  • Filing it away. Review it once a year with your board. Ask which assumptions held, which did not, and what you will change.

Used this way, a theory of change becomes a shared language between funder, grantees and communities, and a practical guide to where the next grant should go.

Where to start

Pick one strategy area, not the whole foundation. Book a half-day session with programme staff and, if possible, two or three grantees and community representatives. Fill in components 1 to 3 together, then let the programme team draft the rest and bring it back for discussion.
