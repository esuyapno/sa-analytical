Beyond Measurement:Why Impact Orientation Matters More Than Impact Metrics

Ekci Suyapno23 November 20267 min read

The measurement paradox

Most international aid organisations collect more data than ever, yet few can confidently say what they would do differently because of it.

Indicator frameworks grow longer each funding cycle. Reports get thicker. Dashboards multiply. And still, when a board asks, “Should we renew this programme?”, the answer often rests on intuition and relationships rather than evidence.

This is the measurement paradox: more measurement has not produced more learning.

Others have made the same point, notably Marco Di Natale in Stanford Social Innovation Review (“Measuring More and Learning Less”), who argues that a focus on methods has distracted from building learning. The problem is rarely a lack of data. Measurement has been treated as an end in itself, a compliance exercise owed to donors, rather than a tool for steering towards results.

The way out is a mindset shift that the sector increasingly calls impact orientation.

Measurement and orientation are not the same thing

Asks: what happened?

Impact measurement

A technical activity: defining indicators, collecting baseline and follow-up data, and assessing whether change happened and whether your work contributed to it.

Asks: what should we do next?

Impact orientation

A way of running an organisation: planning work backwards from the change it is meant to bring about.

The German-speaking sector, where the term Wirkungsorientierung took root, describes impact orientation as planning work backwards from its intended end: what should change, for whom, and why our activities should cause it (PHINEO, Kursbuch Wirkung).

The distinction matters. An organisation can measure diligently and still not be impact-oriented if the data never reaches a decision. Equally, an organisation with modest data can be highly impact-oriented if every activity is tied to a clear logic of change and the team regularly asks whether that logic still holds.

In Switzerland, the idea has moved firmly into the foundation mainstream. SwissFoundations, the association of Swiss grant-making foundations, now runs a dedicated Impact Orientation Working Group for its members.

Why measurement alone falls short

In my earlier work leading assessments alongside local partners and communities, before founding SA Analytical, the same five patterns came up again and again.

  • Counting outputs, calling them outcomes. “5,000 people trained” describes activity, not change. The OECD’s results chain separates outputs (the products and services delivered) from outcomes (the short- and medium-term effects of those outputs). Without a baseline and a follow-up, nobody knows whether anyone’s life is different.
  • Indicators chosen for donors, not decisions. Frameworks are built to satisfy reporting templates. Research cited by the Center for Effective Philanthropy (CEP) found that funders use most of the information they collect from grantees to monitor compliance. The programme team never uses it, so the data arrives late, unread and unloved.
  • Averages that hide the people who matter most. Aggregate figures can mask who is left out. This is why SDG target 17.18 calls for data broken down by income, gender, age, disability, geographic location and other characteristics. Without that breakdown, you rarely know whether you reached the people you set out to reach.
  • Programmes designed for communities, not with them. Funders and head offices often set the indicators and targets. The people a programme serves rarely help define what success looks like, so the data misses the diversity of their needs and priorities.
  • Reports that end the conversation. The annual report is filed, the grant is renewed, and the findings never feed back into strategy. Measurement becomes a ritual rather than a feedback loop.

None of these is a data problem. Each is a problem of purpose: the measurement exists, but no one has decided what it is for.

Five shifts that make an organisation impact-oriented

Impact orientation is less a new toolkit than a set of habits. Five shifts capture most of it.

ShiftFromTo
1Start from the endPlanning activities, then looking for resultsDefining the change you want, then choosing activities
2Make the logic explicitImplicit assumptions about why work “should” helpA written theory of change, with assumptions you can test
3Measure what informs decisionsLong indicator lists for reportingA few indicators tied to real choices
4Look at who is reachedTotals and averagesData broken down by sex, age, location and need, with priorities set together with communities
5Close the loopAnnual reports filed and forgottenRegular review moments where data changes plans

Start from the end. Before asking “What will we do?”, ask “What will be different, and for whom?” This keeps activities in service of change, rather than the other way round.

Make the logic explicit. A theory of change sets out the chain from inputs to long-term impact. Its real value lies in the assumptions between the links, because that is where programmes usually break.

Measure what informs decisions. For every indicator, ask: if this number went up or down, what would we do differently? If the answer is “nothing”, drop it.

Look at who is reached. Disaggregated data turns “we reached 5,000 people” into “we reached 5,000 people, but only 12% were over 60”.* That second sentence is the one that improves a programme. Then go further and ask people directly. Communities know which needs a programme is missing, and their views keep indicators relevant to everyone it is meant to serve.

Close the loop. Build two or three fixed moments a year when the team and board look at the evidence and decide: continue, adapt or stop.

Where to start

You do not need a new data system to become more impact-oriented. Most organisations can begin within a quarter.

  1. Pick one programme. Choose one that faces a real decision in the next 12 months, such as a renewal, a scale-up or a redesign.
  2. Draft its theory of change in a half-day workshop. Bring programme staff, leadership and local partners and, from the start, the people the programme serves. Say the assumptions out loud, then write them down.
  3. Cut the indicator list. Keep three to five that would change the upcoming decision. Add a baseline if you have none.
  4. Disaggregate what you already have. Existing data often holds more than anyone has looked at. Break it down by sex, age and location before collecting anything new.
  5. Schedule the decision meeting now. Put a date in the calendar when the evidence will be reviewed and a choice made. That date gives the data a purpose.

For funders, the same logic applies one level up. Ask grantees fewer, better questions, fund the cost of learning, and treat a programme that changes course in response to evidence as a success, not a failure. If the drive to localise is to be genuine, communities and local organisations need a real say in what gets measured and what counts as success. CEP’s research with grantees points the same way: reporting is most useful when funder and grantee actually discuss it together (CEP, Grantees Report Back).

Measurement in service of decisions

Impact measurement still matters. Without it, impact orientation is just good intentions. But measurement earns its place only when it serves a decision someone is actually going to make, and reflects what matters to the people the work is for.

The organisations that get the most from their data are not the ones that collect the most. They are the ones that know, before collecting anything, what they will do with it.
